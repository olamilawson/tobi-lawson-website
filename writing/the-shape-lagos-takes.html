The Shape Lagos Takes When No One Is Planning It

On informal urbanism, and what the city's edges reveal about how Lagos works.

By Tobi Lawson

Lagos grows in ways no planning document predicts. Walk from Yaba to Ojota and the city changes character block by block. A market spills onto a road. A compound turns half its ground floor into four shops. A drainage channel becomes a footpath and then a street. Almost none of this appears on a masterplan. Most of it works anyway.

Urban planning in West Africa often treats this as a failure of enforcement, a list of violations waiting to be cleared. Watching Lagos build itself reveals a clear logic. The informal city responds directly to three hard facts: rapid population growth, low household incomes, and a state whose formal planning capacity moves at a fraction of the city's speed.

The logic of the informal street

Take the roadside kiosk network. A formal commercial building in Victoria Island requires land title, municipal approval, structural sign-off, and capital before its first door opens. A roadside shop in Mushin requires a wooden frame, agreement with the landholder or local market association, and two weeks of work. One serves the small fraction of the city that can pay high rents. The other serves everyone else.

"The city that gets built is the city residents can afford to build, not the city a masterplan intended."

When planners clear informal commercial spaces without replacing the function they serve, the activity moves two streets down within a month. Demand remains constant even when enforcement arrives.

Designing with the grain

Building urban platforms or infrastructure in Lagos requires tools that work directly with the logic the city already uses.

Transport apps that succeed in Lagos do not force bus drivers to adopt route schedules built for London. They map the routes Danfos run, identify where passengers wait, and build payments around the existing cash collection hierarchy. Water and power solutions that scale build micro-networks around neighborhood clusters that already organize their own security and waste pickup.

Lagos builds itself every morning, one decision at a time. Useful work in the city starts by paying attention to how that building actually happens.
