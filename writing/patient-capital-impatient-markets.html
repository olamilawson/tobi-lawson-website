Patient Capital, Impatient Markets

The timelines investors want and the timelines development requires rarely match. That mismatch has a cost.

By Tobi Lawson

Every venture capital fund operates on the same clock. Money goes in, and within seven to ten years, it needs to return with yield large enough to justify the risk. That clock works well for software, where a good product can find millions of users in months. It works poorly for a company that must build its target market first.

Years spent as an investment analyst before building companies highlighted the mismatch between fund timelines and development timelines. A fintech company in Lagos builds trust in a financial system many potential customers have never used before. It negotiates with regulators writing new rules and waits for smartphone adoption to mature. These steps take longer than a three-to-four-year venture milestone cycle.

Why the mismatch persists

Capital markets reward speed because speed is measurable. A fund reports quarterly growth numbers to its limited partners. It cannot report that a regulatory environment needs six years to mature, even when that fact determines long-term viability. Capital sorts itself toward businesses showing rapid numbers, bypassing companies building foundational conditions.

"Markets price growth quickly. They struggle to price the infrastructure supporting that growth."

Founders building enduring companies often raise smaller rounds, move deliberately, and remain independent longer. Founders free from short-term exit pressure make ten-year decisions instead of eighteen-month decisions. Underfunding remains a constraint, but independence preserves strategic patience.

Where patient capital comes from

Patient capital comes primarily from family offices holding positions across decades, and from founders reinvesting profits into their own balance sheets. Development finance institutions contribute when they move at the speed of opportunity rather than internal bureaucracy.

Building Africa's next generation of large companies requires solving this capital gap. Pitch decks written for short attention spans will not decide the outcome. Aligning capital timelines with institutional building remains the central challenge.
