How Asia Works, and What It Leaves Unanswered for Africa

Joe Studwell's case for land reform and export discipline holds up. The parts that don't translate say a lot about the starting point.

By Tobi Lawson

Joe Studwell's "How Asia Works" demonstrates a clear thesis: Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and China built wealth by breaking standard economic prescriptions. They redistributed land to small farmers before industrializing, forced manufacturers to compete in export markets rather than protecting domestic monopolies, and directed credit toward production instead of speculation.

Studwell supports each claim with concrete historical records. He details specific export quotas set by South Korea's government for chaebols in the 1960s and 1970s, alongside consequences for underperforming firms. He documents how Taiwan redistributed rice paddies to active tillers. These case records give the book lasting weight in development literature.

Where the argument holds for Africa

The core mechanism remains clear: protect domestic production long enough to build capability, then force that production to prove itself in export competition. This sequence applies to any country building an industrial base. Protecting local manufacturing without demanding export performance creates subsidies without expiration dates.

"Protection without an export test is a subsidy with no expiry date."

Where the argument runs into a different Africa

The land reform analysis reflects specific Asian historical conditions. Studwell's cases involved dense agricultural populations working small plots where redistribution increased immediate yields. West Africa presents different agricultural dynamics: lower population density in rural zones, customary land tenure systems, and productivity constraints tied to input access and power infrastructure.

Export discipline requires state administrative capacity to monitor firms against performance metrics. Building that state capacity represents a multi-decade effort in itself.

Studwell's work serves as a practical diagnostic tool rather than a rigid template. Economic development stems from disciplined sector policy adapted to local starting conditions.
